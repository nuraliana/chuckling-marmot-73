# Dad Joke Candidates — Dad Joke Showdown

All 10 candidates were composed from scratch for this bounty. No existing
bounty entries were read, and no jokes were copied from the web or from
famous classics (anti-gravity book, scarecrow award, missing Y, nacho
cheese, skeletons fighting, numbered calendar, robbed coffee, two-tired
bike, "Hi hungry, I'm Dad", chicken crossing the road, eyeless fish,
growing beard, blushing tomato, and all similar variants were avoided).

Scoring per criterion, 1–5. Criteria in judge order:
- **A** Dad joke a father would tell at the dinner table, within 200 chars.
- **B** Pun lands clean — understood on first read, no explanation needed.
- **C** Feels fresh, not told a thousand times, still in the dad-joke tradition.
- **D** Tight — every word earns its place, punchline falls at the end.
- **E** Reads well out loud, a rhythm a dad can deliver at the table.

## Winner: #1 — Thermostat (24/25)

> I told the family the thermostat stays at 72 degrees of separation — between me and anyone who touches it.

**Why it wins:**
- **A (5):** Pure dinner-table dad: the thermostat war is the most universal
  dad-at-home turf battle, and "I told the family" is exactly how a dad
  opens a joke at the table.
- **B (5):** "Degrees of separation" lands on first read — the temperature
  number (72 degrees) and the idiom collide in one phrase, no explanation
  needed, and the em-dash beat delivers the second half cleanly.
- **C (4):** The idiom itself is familiar, but applying it to a thermostat
  setting is a new angle; the joke is not a retread of any known classic.
- **D (5):** 106 characters, zero filler — setup, number, idiom, punchline
  ("between me and anyone who touches it") all land at the very end.
- **E (5):** Natural spoken rhythm: the pause before the dash is where the
  dad grins, and the punchline is short enough to land before anyone
  interrupts.

**Why the runners-up lose:** #2 ("sear-ious") is strong but the pun is
spelled, not spoken — it reads better than it sounds out loud. #3 ("father
figure") spends words on the four-hour setup and the phrase itself is a
familiar idiom. #4 ("Dadabase") needs a half-beat to decode the coined word
and carries a third beat ("nobody can leave") past the punchline. The rest
either stretch the pun ("powerful", "growing on me", "keys") or muddle it
with two half-jokes in one sentence.
